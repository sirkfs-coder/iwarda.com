Saxo Bank’s fee table looks simple at first glance. The headline commissions are clear, but they rarely tell the whole story. If you act on those numbers alone, especially if your trading style doesn’t match the “average” client, it’s easy to end up paying more than you expect.

I’ll break down where traders most often get tripped up, show how to calculate your actual costs, and share a few practical workarounds I’ve learned from tackling these issues head-on.

Minimum Commissions: Small Trades Can Get Expensive Fast

The percentage commission for stocks (like 0.08% for US shares) only applies above a certain trade size. There’s also a minimum charge per trade, often $1 on US stocks. This can make trading small amounts surprisingly costly.

Quick calculation:
For a $150 US stock buy with a $1 minimum, your effective fee is $1 divided by $150, or about 0.67 percent. If you buy $500 worth, it drops to 0.20 percent. Only once you’re well above the minimum does the low advertised rate actually apply.

Illustrative example:
I once worked with an investor who split a $1,000 monthly budget across ten different stocks ($100 each). They expected to pay around 80 cents in total fees (0.08 percent), but actually paid $10 because of the minimums, eating up 1 percent of their investment right away.

How to avoid this:
If you like buying lots of small positions, batch them together into larger trades or consider if another broker with lower (or no) minimums fits better. Always check what the actual fee will be, not just the headline rate.

FX Conversion Spreads: A Quiet Drain on Returns

Buying assets in another currency? Saxo adds a conversion spread (usually around 0.25 percent) every time money moves between currencies, when you buy or sell foreign stocks, not just when funding your account.

What catches people out:
If you’re based in Europe and regularly buy US shares, each purchase and sale involves this extra charge. Over time, frequent conversions add up, sometimes costing more than trading commissions.

Simple check:
List which assets are in a different currency than your main account. For each cross-currency trade, multiply the amount by 0.25 percent (or check Saxo’s current rate).

How to reduce this cost:
If possible, fund your account in the main currency you’ll be trading most often. Some brokers allow sub-accounts in multiple currencies; Saxo has limited options here, so double-check if that matters for you.

Overnight Financing: Hidden Costs for Leveraged Positions

If you hold CFDs or other leveraged products overnight, Saxo charges daily interest based on market rates plus their margin. These rates change with central bank moves and market volatility.

Common mistake:
A trader holds €20,000 in DAX index CFDs for several weeks and expects financing costs to be minor based on old rates. After checking statements later, they find the overnight charges have doubled due to recent rate hikes, a detail missed by relying on outdated info.

Best approach:
Before holding any leveraged position overnight, use Saxo’s financing calculator or contact support for current rates. Don’t guess based on past experience, the numbers can shift quickly.

Account Tiers: Don’t Miss Cheaper Rates by Accident

Saxo offers Classic, Platinum, and VIP accounts; higher tiers bring lower fees but usually require hitting certain annual trading volumes or requesting an upgrade.

Where traders lose out:
Some clients hover just below a volume threshold and stick with Classic fees for months or years without realizing they could qualify for Platinum or VIP rates simply by consolidating trades or asking support directly.

Actionable step:
Add up your expected annual trading volume by asset class and compare it to Saxo’s published requirements for upgrades. If you’re close, or think you should qualify, reach out proactively rather than waiting for an automatic upgrade that may never come.

Comparing Brokers: Go Beyond Headline Rates

When weighing brokers side-by-side, it’s easy to get fixated on commission percentages alone and ignore costs like:

  • Currency conversion spreads
  • Minimum ticket sizes
  • Financing charges
  • Inactivity or custody fees

To get an honest comparison:

  1. Write down your typical trades: size, frequency, asset types.
  2. For each broker (including Saxo), calculate:
  • Per-trade commission (watch minimums)
  • Spread cost (for FX/CFDs)
  • Conversion charge per cross-currency trade
  • Overnight finance costs
  • Any inactivity/withdrawal fees relevant to how you operate
  1. Plug these into a normal month reflecting your actual behavior, not just best-case scenarios.

Example scenario:
Suppose you make five monthly trades in US stocks from a EUR account at $200 each; add up both commissions and conversion charges for every trade before comparing platforms.

Fee Schedule Changes: Stay Alert

Saxo sometimes updates its pricing mid-year without much fanfare. I set myself a calendar reminder every quarter to check their latest fee schedule, and recommend doing the same if your trading patterns change or new products catch your eye.

Practical Next Steps: Run Your Own Real-World Fee Test

The fastest way to spot hidden costs is this: Before funding your account or making new trades,

  • Use Saxo’s online calculators to simulate your top three typical trades.
  • Check where minimums kick in.
  • If anything isn’t clear, such as how many conversions happen per order, ask support with concrete examples before moving money over.

Getting hands-on with these numbers now takes less than an hour but saves far more over months of active investing. Most costly surprises I’ve seen come from skipping this step, not from any single big fee buried deep in fine print.

By running these checks yourself first, you’ll catch expensive surprises early and keep more of what your investing earns as markets move your way.
